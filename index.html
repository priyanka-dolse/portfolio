AI Governance Case Study — Regulatory Roadmap for an Enterprise AI Launch

Written up

EU AI Act · NIST AI RMF · ISO/IEC 42001 · Risk assessment · Stakeholder facilitation

Led a 5-person team producing a governance roadmap for an enterprise software launch, mapping the system against three major AI regulatory and risk frameworks before go-live.

Case study

Context

An enterprise software provider was preparing to launch an AI-assisted feature into an existing product used by regulated business customers. Legal and product teams had a rough sense that "the EU AI Act probably applies," but no shared view of which obligations actually applied, who owned them, or what evidence they'd need to show a regulator or an enterprise customer's procurement team.

Objective

Produce a roadmap the business could actually act on: classify the system's risk tier, translate that into concrete pre-launch obligations, and give engineering and product a sequenced plan rather than a compliance memo that sat in a folder.

Approach

I led a 5-person team through a structured discovery process rather than a desk-based literature review — the framework text alone doesn't tell you what a specific product actually does:

  • Ran structured interviews with product and engineering to map what the AI feature actually did, not what the roadmap deck said it did — the two differed in a few places that mattered for risk classification.
  • Classified the system against the EU AI Act's risk-tier structure, and cross-checked that classification against NIST AI RMF's Govern/Map/Measure/Manage functions to catch anything the EU framework doesn't explicitly ask for but a US enterprise customer likely would.
  • Benchmarked existing internal documentation (model cards, data lineage, testing records) against ISO/IEC 42001's AI management system requirements to find the gaps — this is usually where the real work is, since most of what's "missing" already half-exists somewhere and just needs to be formalised.
  • Facilitated a cross-functional workshop with legal, product and engineering to pressure-test the draft roadmap and get sign-off on ownership for each obligation — a roadmap nobody owns doesn't survive contact with a launch deadline.

Framework alignment (illustrative)

FrameworkWhat it addedRoadmap output
EU AI ActRisk-tier classification & transparency obligationsPre-launch obligations list, owner assigned per item
NIST AI RMFGovern/Map/Measure/Manage lens on top of legal risk tierOngoing monitoring & measurement plan, not just a launch checklist
ISO/IEC 42001Management-system structure — what "good" looks like operationallyGap list against existing documentation, prioritised by effort

Outcome & what I'd do differently

The team delivered a roadmap that reframed governance as a set of owned, sequenced actions rather than a legal opinion — which is what actually got it picked up by engineering. In hindsight, I'd bring engineering into the room a stage earlier: some of the "gaps" we flagged in week three were already half-solved by existing logging infrastructure nobody on the compliance side knew existed.

Employee Attrition Analysis & Prediction

Live

Python · SQL (MySQL) · Logistic Regression · Power BI · DAX

End-to-end HR analytics project on the IBM HR Analytics dataset: schema and data-quality validation in Python, a logistic regression model to find statistically significant attrition drivers, SQL-based KPI validation to keep the numbers honest, and an interactive Power BI dashboard for leadership reporting.

Case study

Problem & hypotheses

1,470 employee records, 16.12% overall attrition. Rather than exploring the data at random, I framed four hypotheses upfront: attrition is higher among early-career employees; lower compensation correlates with higher attrition; overtime and frequent travel raise attrition risk; and attrition falls with higher job level and tenure.

Approach

  • Validated schema and data types, handled missing values by type, and resolved duplicates in Python before any analysis touched the numbers.
  • Ran exploratory data analysis to test each hypothesis, then trained a logistic regression model to identify which drivers were actually statistically significant rather than just visually suggestive.
  • Cross-verified every headline KPI in SQL independently of the Python output, so the dashboard numbers and the model numbers couldn't quietly drift apart.
  • Built the Power BI dashboard last, once the underlying metrics were already validated two different ways.

Findings

All four hypotheses held: entry-level and junior employees, lower income bands, employees under 35, frequent travellers, and staff working overtime with long commutes all showed disproportionately higher attrition. Sales and R&D were the highest-attrition departments.

Recommendation

Short-term: strengthen onboarding/mentorship for early-career staff, review compensation in low-income high-attrition roles, and monitor overtime distribution. Longer-term: revisit travel policy and build targeted retention programmes for Sales and R&D specifically, rather than a blanket company-wide initiative.
